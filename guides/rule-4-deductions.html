Rule 4 protects bookmakers and adjusts odds when horses withdraw from races after betting has begun. For players in the United Kingdom, this industry standard directly reduces potential returns on winning bets in horse racing markets. How Rule 4 Deductions Are Calculated

Rule 4 deduction percentages correspond directly to the odds of the withdrawn runner at the time of removal. Bookmakers use a standardised scale that assigns higher deduction rates to shorter-priced horses. The deduction applies only to the profit portion of a bet, not the returned stake. A £10 bet at 5/1 odds yields £50 profit plus £10 stake. If a 20p in the pound Rule 4 applies, the deduction removes £10 from the profit (20% of £50), returning £40 profit plus the £10 stake for a total of £50.

Withdrawal timing affects deduction severity. Late withdrawals receive the full Rule 4 treatment based on final odds. Earlier removals may qualify for reduced deductions or none at all, depending on when betting commenced. Horse racing markets operate under British Horseracing Authority guidelines that standardise these timings across the United Kingdom. The rule 4 calculator football markets follow similar frameworks adapted to the sport's structure.

Multiple withdrawals compound deductions through successive applications. If two horses are removed, the calculator applies the first deduction, then calculates the second deduction on the already-reduced profit. This cascading effect can substantially diminish returns on races with multiple non-runners. Each withdrawal is processed independently according to that runner's odds.

Odds to Reduction Factor Conversion

Withdrawn horses with odds shorter than 2/1 typically trigger deductions above 50p in the pound. A horse removed at evens (1/1) generates a 75p deduction, removing three-quarters of potential profit. Longer-priced withdrawals produce smaller impacts—a 10/1 removal creates just 15p in the pound deduction. The scale operates in defined bands: odds between 2/1 and 3/1 produce 45p deductions, while 4/1 to 6/1 yields 30p. Odds exceeding 50/1 may trigger no deduction at all, as the withdrawn runner held negligible influence on market probabilities. Horse racing organisers publish these conversion tables, ensuring consistent application across all licensed operators in the United Kingdom.

Multiple Non-Runners and Compounding Deductions

When multiple horses withdraw from the same race, Rule 4 deductions apply sequentially rather than as a simple sum. Each deduction reduces the remaining profit after the previous adjustment has been calculated. A rule 4 calculator processes these compound deductions automatically, preventing manual calculation errors that occur when bettors attempt to add percentages together. The compounding effect means that two 25p deductions do not equal a 50p total reduction—the actual impact on returns is slightly lower due to the sequential application method used across horse racing and football markets.

Step-by-Step Multi-Withdrawal Calculation

Consider a £100 bet on a 5/1 selection with two non-runners: one withdrawn at 7/2 (25p deduction) and another at 9/2 (20p deduction). The original potential profit stands at £500. The first 25p deduction removes £125 (25% of £500), leaving £375 profit. The second 20p deduction then applies to this reduced amount, removing £75 (20% of £375), leaving a final profit of £300. The total deduction equals £200 rather than the £225 that would result from incorrectly adding 25p and 20p together. Each withdrawal triggers a separate calculation stage, with the rule 4 calculator applying the appropriate reduction factor to the profit remaining after previous adjustments.

When Rule 4 Doesn't Apply

Rule 4 deductions do not affect all bet types equally. Ante-post bets placed before final declarations are exempt from Rule 4 protection, meaning stakes are lost entirely if the selection withdraws. Board price bets taken on the day of the event remain subject to standard Rule 4 deductions when non-runners occur. Some bookmakers waive Rule 4 on promotional markets, though these exceptions are clearly stated in terms and conditions.
